What is cash flow forecasting?
A cash flow forecast estimates when cash is expected to enter and leave the business over a future period. Business.gov.au explains that forecasting helps a business assess whether expected income will cover its costs and identify possible shortages or surpluses. Unlike a profit and loss report, the forecast focuses on payment timing and the resulting cash balance.
That timing matters because a business can report an accounting profit while still struggling to meet bills. Sales made on credit, slow-paying customers, inventory purchases, GST and PAYG obligations, loan repayments, equipment purchases and seasonal costs can all create a gap between profit and available cash.
When may a business need cash flow forecasting services?
Cash flow forecasting is useful when future cash availability is uncertain or a decision could materially change receipts and payments. Common situations include:
sales are growing but the bank balance is under pressure
customer payment timing is unpredictable or debtors are increasing
the business has seasonal peaks and quiet periods
large BAS, tax, payroll, superannuation or supplier payments are approaching
new staff, equipment, premises or marketing spend is being considered
finance repayments or other fixed commitments are increasing
the owner wants to understand how long available cash will last under different scenarios
What goes into a useful cash flow forecast?
A forecast should be based on the actual payment patterns of the business rather than simply spreading annual income and expenses evenly across months. Depending on the business, Financially Up may review:
opening bank balances and available cash
expected customer receipts and debtor collection patterns
supplier payments and aged payables
wages, superannuation and payroll-related costs
GST, PAYG instalments, PAYG withholding and other tax payments where applicable
loan, lease and finance repayments
rent, insurance, subscriptions and other recurring overheads
capital expenditure and one-off payments
owner drawings, distributions or other planned cash movements where relevant
The forecast period can vary. A short rolling forecast suits week-by-week cash pressure; a longer monthly forecast supports broader planning. The format should match the decision.
Cash flow forecasting is different from a budget
A budget sets targets for what the business intends to earn and spend. A forecast uses current data, recent trends and updated assumptions to estimate what is likely to happen next. Business.gov.au recommends using the budget to set goals and the forecast to monitor performance and respond to changes.
If your business needs a full planning model covering revenue, costs and performance targets, our budgeting and forecasting service is the more appropriate page. Cash flow forecasting focuses specifically on the timing and availability of cash.

Cash flow and tax obligations

What is the difference between a budget and a forecast?
A budget is the financial plan for what the business wants or expects to achieve over a defined period. A forecast uses current financial data, recent trends and updated assumptions to estimate what is now likely to happen.
Business.gov.au makes the same distinction: a budget helps set goals and direct money, while a forecast helps monitor performance and adjust plans as circumstances change. Used together, they are more useful than preparing a budget once and leaving it untouched.

What should a small business budget include?
The structure depends on the business, but a practical budget will usually cover expected revenue, direct costs, gross profit, employment costs, overheads and other operating expenses. It may also include financing costs, capital expenditure and cash-flow implications where these are relevant to the planning decision.
The assumptions are just as important as the numbers. Revenue may depend on volumes, prices, capacity, customer retention or seasonality. Wages may depend on headcount, hours, salary reviews and payroll on-costs. Rent, software, insurance and other overheads may change at different dates. A credible budget documents these assumptions rather than hiding them inside a spreadsheet.
Financial forecasting should be updated as circumstances change
A forecast should not be treated as a fixed promise. It is an estimate based on available information. As actual results come in, assumptions should be reviewed and the expected outcome updated. This helps owners distinguish between a temporary timing difference and a genuine change in performance.
For example, if revenue is below budget, the next question is why. The cause may be lower sales volume, delayed invoicing, pricing, customer mix, capacity or seasonality. A forecast can then reflect the updated position and show the expected effect on profit and cash.

When might you need a business valuation?
Common reasons include preparing for a sale, considering an acquisition, bringing in a new owner, succession planning or testing whether a proposed price is reasonable. A valuation can also inform discussions with investors or lenders. Each purpose can require a different basis, date, level of investigation and documentation.
The first question is therefore not simply “What is my business worth?” It is “What interest or assets are being valued, for whom, at what date and for what decision?” We agree these points before selecting an approach or requesting extensive records.
How is a business valued?
There is no one calculation suitable for every business. Common approaches consider earnings or cash flow, the value of net assets, or evidence from comparable transactions. The method and assumptions need to fit the business, its industry and the quality of the available information.
An earnings approach may require adjustments for unusual items, owner remuneration, related-party transactions and costs a buyer would actually incur. A cash flow approach depends on supportable forecasts and the risks attached to them. An asset-based approach may be more relevant where significant assets underpin the business or trading earnings do not represent its value. Comparable sale information can be useful, but the businesses and transaction terms must genuinely be comparable.
No formula automatically converts annual profit into sale price. The Australian Government advises gathering business information and considering a suitable valuation method before relying on a figure.
What affects the value beyond the accounts?
Financial statements are the starting point, not the entire picture. Customer concentration, recurring revenue, contracts, lease terms, dependence on the owner, staff retention and quality of records can change how a buyer views earnings. Liabilities, working capital and the assets included in the sale also matter.
For example, a price may assume that stock is included, or that a certain amount of cash and working capital remains at completion. Another offer may price those items separately. We help you compare the basis of each proposal so two headline figures are not mistaken for equivalent offers.

Business valuation FAQs
Can I value a business using a profit multiple?
A multiple can be one input, but it needs a supportable earnings figure and evidence that the multiple fits the business and transaction. A generic industry number is not a valuation on its own.
Is goodwill included?
Goodwill may be part of an overall business value, depending on the method and what is being transferred. It should not be added again if already reflected in an earnings-based calculation.
Do I need a formal independent valuation?

Will a valuation tell me the final sale price?
No. A negotiated price also reflects buyer demand, deal terms, financing and due diligence findings. The valuation provides a reasoned basis for discussion.

What is a business KPI report?

A monthly report might show sales, gross margin, outstanding invoices and cash alongside budget or prior-period figures. What does business performance benchmarking involve?

Why can industry averages mislead?
Two businesses in the same sector may have different products, locations, pricing, hours, staffing, size, customer types and accounting treatments. Published figures also vary in sample, age and definition. Some datasets cover only particular business sizes or industries. We do not present an average as a compulsory target or evidence that a business has underperformed.
