What is included in investment property tax?

When can rental property expenses be deductible?
A deduction generally requires a sufficient connection with earning rental income. For many expenses, the property must be rented or genuinely available for rent and the cost cannot be private or capital in nature. Where an expense relates partly to private use, only the income-producing portion may be deductible.
Common examples can include property-agent fees, council rates, insurance, eligible interest and certain repairs or maintenance. However, the label on an invoice is not decisive. The underlying work and circumstances determine the tax treatment.
Repairs, improvements and initial repairs
Repairs that address wear and tear arising while a property is used to earn rental income may be immediately deductible. By contrast, replacing an entire asset or structure, making an improvement, or fixing defects that existed when the property was acquired can be capital in nature.
This distinction is one of the most common areas of error in investment property tax deductions. Renovation work often contains multiple components, so invoices and scope-of-work documents can be important when determining how the expenditure should be treated.
Interest deductions depend on how borrowed money is used
Interest is not deductible merely because a loan is secured against a rental property. The use of the borrowed funds is central. If part of a loan is redrawn or refinanced for private purposes, the interest may need to be apportioned and the loan history can become difficult to reconstruct later.
Keeping loan statements and records of each redraw or refinance can save substantial time when the rental property tax return is prepared.
Capital works, depreciating assets and records
Some construction expenditure may be deductible over time as capital works. Certain depreciating assets can also be dealt with under decline-in-value rules, although restrictions can apply to second-hand assets in residential rental properties. These rules differ from immediate rental deductions.
A depreciation or quantity-surveyor report can be useful in appropriate circumstances, but it should be applied together with the actual tax rules and property history rather than treated as a substitute for reviewing the records.

Property Subdivision Tax Advice
Subdividing land can change how a later sale is taxed. The outcome depends on why you acquired the property, what you intend to do, the scale and commercial character of the work, and whether the activities amount to an enterprise. A sale may involve capital gains tax (CGT), ordinary income tax, GST or more than one set of rules.
Financially Up can review the property subdivision tax issues before substantial work begins or a sale contract is prepared. Book an Appointment to discuss the ownership history, proposed lots, development work, expected sales and available records.
Does subdividing land trigger tax?
Subdividing one parcel into separate lots does not, by itself, trigger a CGT event while ownership remains unchanged. Each resulting lot becomes a separate CGT asset. It generally retains the acquisition date of the original land, and a later disposal can produce a capital gain or loss.
Subdivision is only the first step in the analysis. The sale proceeds may instead be ordinary income if the land was acquired or the project was undertaken as part of a property-development business or a commercial profit-making transaction. GST is considered under separate rules.
For this reason, the tax on subdividing land in Australia cannot be determined from the number of lots alone. A homeowner selling one unused part of a long-held property may have a different outcome from a person who acquires land, undertakes extensive works and markets multiple lots for profit.
Is the sale taxed under CGT or as ordinary income?
Where subdivided land remains a capital asset, CGT generally applies when a lot is sold. The original land's cost base must be allocated between the new lots on a reasonable basis. Relevant subdivision and sale costs must also be classified and allocated appropriately.
Ordinary income treatment may apply where the activities amount to a business operation or commercial transaction entered into with a profit-making purpose. Relevant facts can include the original and later purpose for holding the land, the change in plans, the level of development, borrowed funds, professional organization, marketing and the owner's involvement.
No single factor decides the result. A change of intention after acquisition can also be important. If an amount is included as ordinary income and a CGT event also arises, the CGT rules contain mechanisms intended to prevent the same economic gain being taxed twice.
How is the cost of the original land divided?
The cost base of the original parcel is divided between the subdivided lots on a reasonable basis. Depending on the facts, an area basis may be reasonable, but relative market values or another method may better reflect materially different lots. The chosen method should be supportable and applied consistently.
Surveying, planning, infrastructure, legal, financing and selling costs do not all receive the same tax treatment. Some may form part of a CGT cost base, some may be deductible or included in the cost of revenue assets, and others may need apportionment. A subdivision tax accountant should review the records rather than treating every invoice as an immediate deduction.
What happens when land beside your home is sold?
Living in a home does not automatically exempt a vacant lot sold separately. The main-residence exemption generally requires the land to be sold as part of the same CGT event as the dwelling and the relevant conditions to be met. A separately sold backyard block will ordinarily require its own tax calculation.
If you intend to retain the existing home and sell the new lot, obtain subdivision tax advice before assuming the sale is exempt. Acquisition history, private and income-producing use, the allocation of cost base, development activity and the sale arrangement may all affect the outcome.
Could GST apply to a subdivided lot?
GST does not apply merely because land is subdivided. It can apply when the sale is made in the course or furtherance of an enterprise, the property is connected with Australia, the seller is registered or required to be registered, and the other requirements for a taxable supply are met. A one-off project can still amount to an enterprise, while a private realization of an asset may not.
If the sale is taxable, GST can affect the contract price, project cash flow, entitlement to GST credits and activity-statement reporting. Sales of new residential premises or potential residential land may also involve purchaser withholding at settlement and supplier notification requirements.
Our GST on Property page explains these separate GST questions in more detail.

Keep records from the original acquisition through to the last sale. Useful documents include:
Purchase contract, settlement statement, stamp duty and legal costs
Subdivision applications, council approvals, surveys and plans
Contemporaneous valuations supporting the allocation between lots
Civil works, demolition, service connections and contractor invoices
Loan, interest and other holding-cost records
Agent agreements, marketing costs, sale contracts and settlement statements
GST registrations, activity statements and tax invoices, where relevant
Records showing when, why and how the plan for the property changed
Records relevant to a CGT calculation should generally be kept for at least five years after the relevant CGT event, and longer where they are needed to establish the cost base of land not yet sold.

Property subdivision tax FAQs
Is tax payable when I divide land into two titles?
Subdivision alone generally does not trigger a CGT event if ownership does not change. Tax consequences commonly arise when a lot is sold, although GST registration, project accounting and record-keeping issues may need attention earlier.
Is the sale of a subdivided block always a capital gain?
No. Depending on the purpose, scale and commercial character of the activities, sale proceeds may be ordinary income rather than solely a capital gain. GST must be assessed separately.
Does the main-residence exemption cover a vacant lot sold separately?
Generally, no. The exemption for adjacent land ordinarily depends on the land being disposed of with the eligible dwelling as part of the same CGT event and on the other conditions being met.

Seek advice before substantial works begin or contracts are prepared. At that stage, the intended activity, GST position, record keeping and expected tax treatment can be reviewed while commercial decisions remain open.

Property Capital Gains Tax Advice
Selling property can create a capital gain or capital loss, but the difference between the purchase price and sale price is not the complete calculation. Acquisition and selling costs, improvements, prior deductions, ownership, residency and the property's use must all be considered.
Financially Up provides property capital gains tax calculation, reporting and separately scoped advice for Australian property owners. Book an Appointment before settlement or tax-return preparation to review the transaction and available records.
When does property capital gains tax apply?
A disposal of property acquired on or after 20 September 1985 generally triggers a CGT event. For a standard sale contract, the event usually occurs when the parties enter into the contract, not when settlement occurs. The capital gain or loss is therefore reported in the income year of the contract date, even if settlement is later.
A qualifying main residence may be fully exempt. An investment property, holiday home, vacant land or property used to produce income will generally require a CGT calculation. Special rules can apply to inherited property, relationship breakdowns, pre-CGT interests and changes in tax residency.
A sale may instead produce ordinary income where the property was acquired or developed as part of a business or commercial profit-making transaction. Our Property Subdivision Tax page explains the distinct issues arising when land is divided or developed for sale.
How is a property capital gain calculated?
Broadly, sale proceeds are compared with the property's cost base. The calculation then applies the relevant CGT rules, including capital losses and any available discount. It is important to follow the correct order: eligible current-year capital losses and carried-forward net capital losses are generally applied before an eligible CGT discount.
The cost base can include eligible amounts paid to acquire, hold, improve, preserve and sell the property. Common examples include the purchase price, stamp duty, conveyancing costs, agent commission and qualifying capital improvements. The treatment depends on the facts, and an amount is not included simply because it appears on an invoice.
Capital works deductions can reduce the cost base or reduced cost base where the law requires an adjustment. This can include deductions already claimed and, in some circumstances, amounts that could still be claimed. Depreciating assets may also require separate treatment rather than being included in the property CGT calculation.
Is the CGT discount available?
Individuals and trusts may generally access the CGT discount for an eligible asset held for at least 12 months. The acquisition and disposal dates, type of owner and other conditions must be checked. Companies cannot use the general CGT discount.
Foreign-residency periods can restrict the discount, and foreign residents are generally not entitled to the main-residence exemption unless a specified life-event exception applies. Residency history should therefore be identified early rather than dealt with after the gain has been calculated.
What if the property was once your home?
A dwelling can qualify for a full or partial main-residence exemption, depending on ownership, occupancy, income-producing use and other circumstances. Renting out all or part of the property, operating a business from it, delaying occupation or treating another dwelling as a main residence can change the outcome.
The absence rule may allow a former home to continue being treated as a main residence for up to six years while it is used to produce income, or indefinitely during an absence when it is not used to produce income. Conditions and choices about another home apply; it is not an automatic six-year exemption for every rental property.
If a home is first used to produce income, the market-value rule may reset the relevant acquisition value at that time, but only if its conditions are satisfied. In broad terms, the property must have qualified for a full main-residence exemption immediately before that first income-producing use. The original cost must not automatically be replaced by a valuation.
What if you are selling an investment property?
For an investment property, we review acquisition and sale dates, legal ownership, rental history, capital improvements, depreciation and capital works schedules, and prior deductions. Joint owners generally calculate and report their respective interests; payment of expenses by one owner does not necessarily change legal ownership.

Is GST payable when property is sold?
A property sale is generally taxable only when made for consideration in the course or furtherance of an enterprise, connected with Australia, and the seller is registered or required to be registered for GST. An exemption or input-taxed treatment may alter the result.
A private sale of a family home will ordinarily be different from a sale made through a property-development enterprise. However, a single project can still amount to an enterprise. The owner's purpose, activities, organization, scale and commercial character must be considered rather than relying only on the number of transactions.
GST registration and turnover require careful analysis for property projects. The sale price can be significant, but not every property disposal is treated identically for projected GST turnover. Obtain advice before assuming that being unregistered means GST cannot apply.
Existing and new residential premises
The sale or long-term rental of existing residential premises is generally input taxed. The seller does not charge GST, and acquisitions relating to those input-taxed supplies generally do not give rise to GST credits.
A taxable sale can arise for new residential premises. Premises may be new because they have not previously been sold as residential premises, were created through substantial renovations, or were built to replace demolished premises on the same land. Building age alone is not a complete test.
Residential premises that would otherwise be new may cease to be new after being used solely to make input-taxed residential rental supplies for a continuous period of at least five years. The precise history and statutory conditions matter. Commercial residential premises, such as qualifying hotels or similar accommodation, follow different GST rules.

What is the GST margin scheme?
The margin scheme is an alternative way to calculate GST on an eligible taxable sale of real property. It does not make the sale GST-free and the margin is not simply the accounting profit. Eligibility and the calculation depend on how and when the seller acquired the property and the applicable valuation or consideration rules.
The margin scheme is not available for every sale. For example, the seller's acquisition history may prevent its use. The seller and purchaser must generally agree in writing to apply the scheme on or before the supply is made, subject to limited ATO discretion to allow more time.

What does negative gearing mean for tax?
A property is commonly described as negatively geared when the income it earns is less than the deductible costs associated with earning that income. For tax purposes, the key number is the net rental result calculated under the tax rules, not simply the difference between rent received and mortgage repayments.
Loan principal repayments are not a rental deduction. Interest may be deductible to the extent the borrowed money was used for an income-producing purpose and the other requirements are met. Some property costs are immediately deductible, while others may need to be claimed over time or included in the property’s CGT cost base.

Rental losses are based on deductible expenses, not cash flow
A common mistake is to treat every property payment as a tax deduction. Tax treatment depends on what the expense is for. Interest, council rates, agent fees, insurance and certain operating costs may be deductible where the property is rented or genuinely available for rent and the relevant conditions are met. Capital expenditure, loan principal and private costs are treated differently.

Property expenditure needs to be classified correctly. Some repairs and maintenance may be deductible when they relate to ordinary wear or damage arising while the property is producing income. When is a home generally fully exempt from CGT?
A dwelling can generally qualify for a full main residence exemption when you satisfy the residency requirements, it has been the home of you and your family for the whole ownership period, it has not been used to produce assessable income, and the other requirements are met. The exemption can extend to the dwelling and up to two hectares of associated land used for private purposes. Different outcomes can apply to larger areas, income-producing land and foreign residents.
Whether a property was genuinely your main residence is determined from the circumstances. Relevant facts can include where you and your family lived, your personal belongings, mailing address, electoral enrolment, utilities and the overall pattern of occupation. There is no single document that automatically decides the issue.
When can the main residence exemption become partial?
A partial exemption can arise where the property was rented before you lived in it, part of the home was used to earn rental or business income, you moved out and did not choose or qualify to continue treating it as your main residence, the income-producing absence exceeded the available period, or another property was treated as your main residence for an overlapping period.
The calculation can depend on the number of non-main-residence days, the proportion of the property used to produce income and special rules that alter the normal cost base. A main residence CGT accountant can reconstruct the timeline and determine which rules need to be considered.
Renting out a former home
If you move out of a home that was your main residence and later rent it, you may be able to continue treating it as your main residence for CGT purposes for a limited period while it produces income. This is commonly called the six-year rule.
The separate 6-Year Rule page explains that absence rule in detail. This main-residence page focuses on the broader CGT exemption, including cases that do not fit the six-year rule or that involve only a partial exemption.
The home first used to produce income rule
Where a home first starts being used to produce income after 20 August 1996, the statutory market-value rule must be considered. Broadly, it applies where a later sale would be only partly exempt and a full main residence exemption would have been available if the home had been sold immediately before its first income-producing use. When all conditions are met, the home is treated as acquired at its market value at that time for the later CGT calculation.
This rule does not apply in every change-of-use case. For example, it operates differently where a property was rented from the time it was acquired or where the property remains fully exempt because you validly continue treating it as your main residence. Because the valuation date can materially affect a later CGT calculation, it is important to keep an appropriate valuation when the change occurs.
Buying another home before selling the old one
There are limited rules that can allow two dwellings to be treated as a main residence for an overlapping period when you are moving from one home to another and the conditions are met. Outside those rules, you generally cannot treat two properties as your main residence for the same period.
Where there is a long overlap, a former home is rented, or you are choosing which property should receive the exemption for a period, the CGT outcome should be reviewed before the tax return is prepared.
Using part of your home to earn income
Using part of a home to produce assessable income can affect the exemption for that part. This may arise with a separately rented room, short-stay accommodation, or part of the property used as a place of business where interest on the relevant borrowing could be deductible.
Occasionally working from home does not automatically create the same CGT result as using a dedicated part of the property as a place of business. The actual use of the area, the period of use and the relevant tax tests need to be considered.
Main residence CGT on a property rented before you move in
If a property is rented before it ever becomes your main residence, the main residence exemption generally does not cover that earlier rental period. Moving in later can start a period of main-residence eligibility, but it does not usually convert the prior investment period into exempt ownership.
This is one reason the full ownership timeline matters. Purchase contract, settlement, tenant dates, move-in dates, periods of absence and the sale contract date may all be relevant.
Tax residency can affect the exemption
The main residence exemption is subject to specific rules for foreign residents. If you were a foreign resident when the CGT event occurred, or your residency changed during ownership, the exemption should not be assumed. Limited exceptions can apply in particular circumstances, so residency dates and the sale contract date should be reviewed as part of the CGT analysis.

How does the six-year rule work?
When a property stops being your actual home, you can in some circumstances choose to continue treating it as your main residence for CGT purposes. If the former home is used to produce income, such as by renting it out, that continued treatment is generally limited to six years for each relevant absence period.
If the property is not used to produce income after you move out, the continuing main residence treatment may apply for an unlimited period, subject to the other rules. The six-year limit specifically matters when the former home is income-producing.
The property generally needs to have been your home first
The six-year rule applies to a former main residence. If a property was acquired and rented out from the beginning, later moving into it does not make the earlier rental period retrospectively exempt. Once it genuinely becomes your main residence, a later absence may potentially qualify under the rule.
This distinction is important for investors who buy a property with tenants in place and move in years later. A 6 year CGT rule accountant should separate the investment period before occupancy from any later period that may qualify for the absence rule.
You generally cannot claim two main residences for the same period
If you choose to continue treating your former home as your main residence after moving out, you generally cannot also treat another property as your main residence for the same period, apart from limited overlap rules when changing homes.
This can create a genuine choice where you move into another property while retaining and renting the old home. The best CGT outcome cannot be assumed from property values alone; acquisition dates, expected ownership periods, rental use and future sale plans can all matter. Tax planning should be considered before the sale return is prepared where a choice is available.
What happens after six years of rental use?
If a single income-producing absence lasts more than six years and you do not re-establish the property as your main residence, the continued exemption generally stops after the six-year period. A later sale may therefore be partly exempt and partly taxable.
The taxable portion is not necessarily calculated by simply taxing all growth after the six-year anniversary. The relevant main-residence and cost-base rules, including any applicable market-value rule, need to be considered together.
Can the six-year period restart?
Where you move back into the property and genuinely re-establish it as your main residence, then later move out again, a new income-producing absence can potentially have its own six-year period. Whether the property has genuinely become your main residence again depends on the facts.
There is no simple rule that a short visit automatically resets the clock. Evidence of genuine occupation and the overall circumstances should support the position.
The home first used to produce income valuation can still matter
When a former home is first used to produce income after 20 August 1996, the home-first-used-to-produce-income rule must be considered. If its conditions are met and the eventual sale is only partly exempt, the home is treated as acquired at market value when it first became income-producing. A contemporaneous valuation can therefore be important to the later CGT calculation.
However, where the former home remains fully exempt because the six-year rule covers the entire relevant rental period, the special market-value rule may not affect the final gain in the same way. The facts should be reviewed before relying on a valuation or ignoring one.
6 year rule and a property sold within six years
If you lived in the property as your main residence, moved out, rented it and sold it within six years of income-producing use, you may be able to choose to treat it as your main residence for the whole absence. If the conditions are satisfied and no conflicting main-residence choice is made, this can result in a full exemption for that period.
The actual CGT event for a sale generally occurs when the sale contract is entered into, not at settlement. That date can matter when measuring the relevant period and identifying the income year for reporting.

Potentially. If you genuinely re-establish the property as your main residence and later move out again, a new absence period may qualify. The occupation needs to be genuine and supported by the facts.
What if I rent the former home for more than six years?
A partial exemption may apply. The period after the available six-year income-producing absence can become taxable, and the correct calculation may also involve special main-residence and market-value rules.

What is a property ownership structure?
A property ownership structure identifies who legally holds the property and, where different, who has the beneficial interest. Common arrangements include ownership by an individual, joint owners, a company or a trustee. Each option has different income tax, capital gains tax, reporting, administration and succession consequences.
There is no universally best structure. An arrangement that suits a long-term residential rental may be unsuitable for a development project or premises used by a family business. The intended use, funding and exit strategy should be clear before the options are compared.
Owning property personally or with another person
Individual ownership is familiar to many investors. Rental income and allowable deductions are generally reported by the owner. For co-owned rental property, income and expenses generally follow each owner's legal interest, rather than an informal agreement about who pays the bills. Limited exceptions can apply where the beneficial ownership is genuinely different, so the title and supporting documents matter.
A later sale may produce a capital gain or loss for each owner. An individual may qualify for the CGT discount where the relevant conditions are met, including the applicable ownership period. The main residence exemption is a separate issue that depends on the facts and should not be assumed for an investment property.

Could a trust own the property?
A trustee can acquire a property for a trust where the deed, trustee powers and other legal requirements allow it. Trusts may offer flexibility in some circumstances, but they add administration and their tax outcomes depend on the type of trust, the deed, the beneficiaries and the transactions undertaken. Income distributions, trust losses and capital gains require careful treatment.
An eligible trust may access the CGT discount on a qualifying gain, but the ultimate result can depend on the beneficiary and how the gain is dealt with. Trust tax losses generally remain in the trust and can only be used later if the relevant rules are satisfied. Trust ownership does not guarantee lower tax, and state or territory land tax and transfer duty rules must also be considered.

Can an SMSF buy property?
Yes, subject to the fund's trust deed, investment strategy and superannuation law. Trustees must consider the sole purpose test, arm's length terms, market value and clear fund ownership. They also need to assess investment risk, liquidity, diversification, expected return, liabilities and the fund's ability to pay expenses and member benefits.
The investment decision must be made for members' retirement interests. Buying property so a member or relative can use it personally is inconsistent with that purpose and can breach other investment restrictions. Financially Up can explain tax and accounting consequences; recommending whether to buy, sell or hold a particular property may require licensed financial product advice.
Residential property and related parties
Residential property held directly by an SMSF generally cannot be used as a home or holiday property by a member or related party. Charging market rent does not, by itself, make personal or related-party residential use permissible. Acquiring an asset from a related party is also restricted and should be reviewed before contracts are exchanged.
Business real property can qualify for specific exceptions, including certain acquisitions from and leases to related parties, where all legal conditions are met and the dealings are on commercial terms. Broadly, the property's underlying use must satisfy the statutory business-use test. The label 'commercial property' is not enough, and mixed-use or unusual arrangements require careful review.
What changed for SMSF property borrowing in August 2026?
SMSFs are generally prohibited from borrowing, subject to limited exceptions. A limited recourse borrowing arrangement (LRBA) is a specialized exception with strict asset-holding and financing requirements. For an LRBA entered into on or after 10 August 2026 to finance real property acquired after that date, the property must be business real property. This materially limits new financed residential property acquisitions by SMSFs.
The change does not apply in the same way where the SMSF exchanged a binding contract to acquire the real property before 10 August 2026. Existing LRBAs entered into before that date and refinancing of those arrangements also have transitional treatment. The conditions should be checked against the documents and timing; trustees should not assume an older example of an SMSF borrowing to buy a residential rental remains available for a new transaction.
An SMSF may still be able to acquire residential property without borrowing if the acquisition and ongoing ownership otherwise comply with the superannuation rules. The holding trustee, title, security, permitted expenditure and terms of any loan require coordinated legal, lending, tax and accounting review.
Tax and reporting during ownership
Rental income, expenses and any later disposal are recorded and reported by the SMSF. Deductions depend on the character of the expenditure and the fund's circumstances. Repairs, improvements, borrowing costs and capital expenditure can receive different treatment. CGT, GST and changes in the fund's tax position can affect the result, so no tax rate or saving should be assumed without reviewing the facts.
Transactions with related parties must be conducted on arm's length terms. Non-arm's length arrangements can have serious tax consequences, and evidence such as market valuations, commercial lease terms and proof of payment may be important. Property development, major alterations and related-party finance can introduce additional issues beyond ordinary rental reporting.
